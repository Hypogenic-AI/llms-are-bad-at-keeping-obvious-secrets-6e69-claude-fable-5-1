# Notes: Creating Suspenseful Stories: Iterative Planning with Large Language Models (arXiv 2402.17119v1)

Kaige Xie, Mark Riedl (Georgia Tech). 27 Feb 2024. Read in full: 17 pages, main text + Appendix A (genre list), B (detail-elaboration prompts and example event sequences), C (full example story).

## 1. Research question

Can an LLM be made to write *suspenseful* stories zero-shot, by decomposing generation into an explicit, theory-grounded planning procedure, when direct prompting does not reliably produce suspense? Secondary: which factors (clues, timing of information revelation, reader empathy) affect perceived suspense?

Theoretical grounding:
- Gerrig & Bernardo (1994): readers feel suspense when the protagonist faces a negative outcome and "the quantity or quality of feasible means of escaping the impending negative outcome has become diminished".
- Disparity of knowledge (Branigan, 2013): "When a character knows something that the reader doesn't know, the revelation of that information can invoke surprise in the reader. However, when the reader knows something that the story world characters don't know, and that knowledge is related to the protagonist's chances of a negative outcome, then the reader may feel suspense." So "a key consideration in suspense generation is reasoning about what characters know versus what readers know."

Authors' claim about LLMs: "suspense is a complex cognitive phenomenon that does not emerge in the latent state representations of a transformer. Instead, one must infer the cognitive state of the reader, or induce a narrative structure with known properties that co-occur with suspense." (asserted, not tested mechanistically). Also asserted without reported experiment: "Model fine-tuning on suspenseful story corpora does not result in an LLM that is able to capture the latent conditions necessary for the reader to feel suspense."

## 2. Methodology

### Models
- Main: ChatGPT `gpt-3.5-turbo-0613`.
- Transfer check: Llama 2 `Llama-2-13b-chat`.
- Every dialogue session starts with: "You are a creative storyteller."
- For non-story intermediate outputs they append when necessary: "Use no more than [n] sentences in your answer." (n = three, four, or five).
- Temperature / sampling settings: not stated. Story length statistics: not stated. Number of planning iterations used in the evaluated stories: not stated explicitly (the worked example and the ablation prompts use three actions with two failure reasons; "A complete run ... may contain two or more prompting iterations").

### Pipeline: Iterative-Prompting-based Planning (three stages)

**Stage 1, Background Setup (Sec 3.1, Table 1).** Sample a genre from an LLM-produced list of 50 thriller genres (Appendix A). Then prompts, verbatim:
1. "Tell me about the name and the occupation of a protagonist of a spy thriller." -> `$name_occupation`
2. "Tell me about a very concrete goal the protagonist wants to achieve." -> `$goal`
3. "Tell me about a dire situation that the protagonist would be put in, if the protagonist couldn't achieve the goal." -> `$dire_situation`
4. "Write an introductory paragraph of a story that covers all the above information."

**Stage 2, Outline Planning (Sec 3.2, Table 2).** Iterate action -> failure reason. Verbatim prompts:
```
Protagonist's Name and Occupation: $name_occupation
Protagonist's Goal: $goal
Protagonist's Dire Situation (if the goal is not achieved): $dire_situation
Tell me about a concrete action the protagonist is most likely to take, in order to achieve the goal.
```
-> `$action1`
```
The protagonist tries to perform the following action: $action1. But the protagonist still fails to achieve the goal for a reason. Tell me about what this reason could be.
```
-> `$reason1`
```
The protagonist has taken all the following actions: [$action1]. But it turns out that those actions are not effective in helping the protagonist to achieve the goal, so the goal is still not achieved yet. Tell me about a concrete new action the protagonist is most likely to take next, in order to achieve the goal.
```
-> `$action2`, then the reason prompt again -> `$reason2`, and so on. Each action is conditioned on the failure of all previous actions; "most likely" is used so that actions decrease in likelihood (a rational protagonist tries best options first). In the final iteration no failure reason is generated; the protagonist succeeds. No limit on iterations.

**Stage 3, Detail Elaboration (Sec 3.3, Appendix B Table 7).** Each (action, reason) pair becomes a chapter summary, then an event sequence (bullet list), then prose. Verbatim prompt for a failing chapter:
```
Elaborate the following story summary into a story chapter by writing a sequence of events for me.
Make sure the protagonist is not aware of the reason until the action is taken.
Make sure to reveal the reason for the action's ineffectiveness in late stages of the story.
Make sure to set up some small clues that can suggest the protagonist's action's ineffectiveness.
Story Summary: The protagonist tries to take the following action: $action1. But it does not work due to the following reason: $reason1.
Story Chapter (i.e. a sequence of events):
```
Final (success) chapter summary line:
```
Story Summary: The protagonist takes the following action: $action3. Therefore, the protagonist successfully achieves the goal: $goal. The story ends with the protagonist's success.
```
"With a sequence of events, the full story is then generated by asking the LLM for an elaboration on the event details." The exact prompt for this last events-to-prose step is **not given**.

Two optional designs (both exposed as prompt lines above):
1. **Clue setup**: "we ask the LLM to set up some small clues for the upcoming failure of the protagonist's action so as to better arouse readers' anticipation".
2. **Information revelation mode**: reveal the failure reason *beforehand* (reader knows, protagonist does not) or *after the fact*. The printed prompt shows the "late stages" variant; the exact wording of the "beforehand" variant is not given.

Important constraint the authors impose: "During prompting we need to make sure the protagonist is not aware that their plan will fail".

### Human evaluation (Sec 4)
- Platform: Prolific; IRB-approved; US-located, English first language; screening question; distinct participant pool per study; inter-annotator agreement via Fleiss's kappa (described as "fair"; **numeric kappa values not stated**).
- Pairwise comparison questions (verbatim):
  - Suspense: "Which story makes you feel a higher level of suspense?"
  - Novelty: "Which story do you find to be more novel?"
  - Enjoyment: "Which story is more enjoyable to read?"
  - Logical Sense: "Which story logically makes more sense?"
  - Naturalness: "Which story is more likely to have been written by a human?"
- Significance: Wilcoxon sign test.
- Design per study: 90 participants, 30 story pairs, each participant rates 10 pairs, each pair rated by 30 participants. Stories randomly selected from "a corresponding large collection" (collection size not stated).

## 3. Datasets and sizes
- No external story corpus used (fully zero-shot).
- Evaluated material: 30 story pairs per comparison; 30 stories for outline examination and for empathy study.
- Appendix A: list of 50 suspense genres (e.g. Psychological Thriller, Crime Thriller, Mystery Thriller, Spy Thriller, Heist Thriller, Betrayal Thriller, Identity Thriller, ...).
- Appendix C: one full generated example story (spy thriller, "Max Sterling" vs "The Phoenix", intro + 3 chapters).
- No generated-story dataset is released.

## 4. Baselines
1. **Direct prompting** (same LLM, same background): "Story Background: $name_occupation. $goal. Write a full suspenseful story based on the story background."
2. **Llama 2 direct prompting** (same setup with Llama-2-13b-chat for both methods).
3. **Re3** (Yang et al., 2022b), long-form story generator, conditioned on the same background.
4. **Ablation #1** (no detail elaboration; whole outline in one prompt), verbatim:
```
Story Background: $name_occupation. $goal.
Action #1: $action1
Reason #1: $reason1
Action #2: $action2
Reason #2: $reason2
Action #3: $action3
Story Summary: Aiming to achieve the goal: $goal, the protagonist first tries to take Action #1, but fails due to Reason #1; then tries to take Action #2, but fails due to Reason #2; finally tries to take Action #3 and this time the protagonist successfully achieves the goal.
Write a full suspenseful story based on the story summary.
```
5. **Ablation #2** (additionally no outline planning; only the abstract structure), verbatim:
```
Story Background: $name_occupation. $goal.
Story Summary: Aiming to achieve the goal: $goal, the protagonist first tries to take a first action, but fails due to a reason; then tries to take another new action, but fails due to another reason; finally tries to take a new action and this time the protagonist successfully achieves the goal.
Write a full suspenseful story based on the story summary.
```

## 5. Metrics
Human pairwise preference Win% / Lose% / Tie% on Suspense, Novelty, Enjoyment, Logical Sense, Naturalness; outline-validity percentages; 1-5 Likert for empathy and suspense. No automatic metrics.

Outline examination (Sec 4.3; 30 participants per aspect, 30 stories):
- Protagonist actions relevant to goal: 96.5% of the time.
- Plausible reason for failure: 89.8%.
- Likelihood of successive actions perceived as decreasing: 55.1%; increasing 13.9%; same 31.0%.

Factors affecting suspense (Sec 5; 90 participants, 30 pairs each for the first two):
- **Clue setup**: with clues judged more suspenseful by 57.9%, without clues 10.9%, tie 31.2% (significant, p < 0.05). "clues of impending failure are a factor that increases reader perceptions of suspense."
- **Revelation timing**: reason revealed beforehand more suspenseful 42.1%, after the fact 36.3%, tie 21.6%; **not statistically significant**.
- **Empathy** (30 participants, 30 stories, 1-5 scales): high suspense usually co-occurs with high empathy. Heatmap counts (rows suspense 5..1, columns empathy 1..5): S5: 11, 26, 49, 43, 89; S4: 23, 18, 30, 63, 75; S3: 31, 43, 56, 52, 34; S2: 25, 38, 22, 19, 8; S1: 71, 31, 24, 15, 4.

## 7. Limitations
Stated by authors: English only; grounded in Western storytelling tradition; other languages would need prompt engineering and new human evaluation.

Evident from what is (not) reported:
- Human preference only; no automatic or objective measure, and nothing that measures information leakage or premature revelation.
- Numeric Fleiss kappa not given; number of planning iterations, sampling temperature, story lengths, and size of the story pool not stated.
- Models are gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 and Llama-2-13b-chat only.
- The mechanistic claim (suspense "does not emerge in the latent state representations of a transformer") is not tested.
- The final events-to-prose prompt and the "reveal beforehand" prompt variant are not printed.
- No code or data release mentioned.

Our observation on the paper's own example (Appendix B/C), relevant to our hypothesis: despite the instructions "Make sure the protagonist is not aware of the reason until the action is taken" and "reveal the reason ... in late stages", the Chapter 1 event list states the reason at bullet 4 of 7, mid-chapter and before the consequence plays out: "a feeling of unease began to creep in. There was something off about the atmosphere in the room. Unbeknownst to Max, a double agent within their own agency had betrayed them." The paper does not comment on or measure this; it is one example, and the protagonist-unaware constraint is technically kept (narrator tells the reader). It illustrates how the model handles "hold this back until late" with narrator-level telegraphing.

## 9. Reusable for our experiment

**Outline / plan formats (directly reusable as our "explicit plan" condition)**
- Three plan granularities that already form a graded manipulation of how much plan is externalised:
  1. No plan: direct prompt ("Write a full suspenseful story based on the story background.").
  2. Abstract structure only (Ablation #2 prompt).
  3. Concrete outline in one prompt (Ablation #1 prompt: Action/Reason list + Story Summary).
  4. Full hierarchical pipeline: outline -> per-chapter event bullet list -> prose, one chapter per call.
  We can adopt the same ladder with a secret in place of the failure reason and measure leakage at each rung.
- The `$reasonK` slot is structurally a secret: information fixed in the plan that must not surface (to the protagonist) until a specified point. The elaboration prompt lines ("Make sure the protagonist is not aware of the reason until the action is taken." / "Make sure to reveal the reason ... in late stages of the story.") are ready-made withholding instructions. Replacing "set up some small clues" with "do not hint at it" gives a matched no-foreshadowing variant; the clue line itself is an explicit, controllable foreshadowing knob.
- Chapter-wise generation means the prose-writing call for chapter k can be given only the events of chapter k. This offers an external-plan condition where the secret is *not in the context at all* while early prose is written, the cleanest behavioural test of whether leakage needs the secret to be present in context.

**Story prompts / seeds**
- Background-setup prompts (name+occupation, concrete goal, dire situation, intro paragraph) and the 50-genre list give a cheap, zero-shot generator of diverse story premises; "Mystery Thriller", "Betrayal Thriller", "Identity Thriller", "Spy Thriller" naturally contain a hidden fact (culprit, traitor, identity).
- The Max Sterling example (double agent betrayal) is a ready test item for a "who is the mole" secret.
- System line "You are a creative storyteller." and the "Use no more than [n] sentences in your answer." length control.

**Evaluation protocol**
- Pairwise Win/Lose/Tie with Wilcoxon sign test, 30 pairs x 30 raters; question wordings above. For our API-judge setting we can reuse the five question wordings with an LLM judge and add a leakage-specific question (e.g. can a reader guess the secret from the first N% of the story), which this paper does not have.
- Outline-validity checks (relevance, plausibility) as sanity checks on generated plans.
- Controlling for content: baseline and method are conditioned on the same background information, so differences come from the planning procedure. We should likewise hold premise and secret fixed across plan conditions.

**Implication for "can an external plan offload internal planning?"**
- Behavioural evidence that it helps with a narrative property that depends on managing who knows what and when: externalising the plan (outline, then per-chapter events) raised suspense preference from the direct-prompt baseline at every step of the ladder (72.3% -> 79.8% -> 84.9% win vs baseline; full vs Ablation #1 75.0%, full vs Ablation #2 80.4%). The authors' reading is that once the problem is decomposed, "the LLM is capable of generating all intermediate information required".
- It is not evidence about secret-keeping as such. The method is built to create reader anticipation; clues *increase* perceived suspense (57.9% vs 10.9%), and revealing the reason to the reader beforehand was, if anything, preferred (42.1% vs 36.3%, n.s.). So in this genre some "leakage" to the reader is desirable, and LLM over-foreshadowing may partly reflect a learned narrative convention. Our experiments should distinguish instructed foreshadowing from uninstructed leakage of a secret the model was told to keep.
- The paper has no internal-state measurements; it cannot say whether the plan changes what the model represents while writing. It motivates our condition (a) and supplies the plan formats; the mechanism question (b) is untouched by it.
